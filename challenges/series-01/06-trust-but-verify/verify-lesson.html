Lesson 6 · judging what comes back

Trust, but verify

A wrong answer arrives in the same confident voice as a right one. Today you learn where to be suspicious — and where you're allowed to relax.

Confident is not correct

When the model is wrong, nothing in its voice tells you. The fluent, sure register is identical either way — there's no tone-of-voice warning, and no clever phrasing removes it. That's not a defect to fix; it's a property of the technology to work with. You just met it, in your own field.

The risk is jagged — and that's good news

Low risk
working over material you supplied
High risk
recalled specifics — citations, statistics, dates, niche facts

Summarising your document: low. Naming a study from memory: high. Once you can read this map, suspicion stops being a mood and becomes calibration — you know exactly which of your tasks deserve the hard look.

Two moves that beat it

  • Open the source. Cited is not verified — an unopened citation is a claim wearing a costume. Open it and see whether it says what it's said to say.
  • Make it compute. For numbers: "don't estimate — write the calculation, run it, show your working." Same colleague, two modes; a computed answer can be rerun and audited, a guess can't.
A fluent estimate is more dangerous than an obviously wrong one — plausible is what invites trust.

Calibrate it to stakes

Always check
names, numbers, quotes leaving under your name
Sometimes
mid-stakes drafts — spot-check what's load-bearing
Never worry
brainstorms, throwaways — skip, guilt-free

"Verify everything" is as unusable as "trust everything." Your checklist earns its keep by naming what you're allowed to skip as clearly as what you must check.

Lesson 6 · your activity

Catch it, then build your checklist

Two probes on your own ground, two countermeasures back home — and you leave with a checklist calibrated to your work.

  1. Break out fresh. Click + New task (top-left) — no folder, just a plain conversation, away from your course. (Already ran the probes with your guide? Bring both answers to step 4.)
  2. Probe your field. Ask about what you know best, escalating from basics to the niche — named studies, regulations, statistics, dates — and ask it to name its sources. Hunt until something arrives that you, the expert, can catch — or can't verify.
  3. Probe your numbers. Paste a few real figures (stand-ins for anything private) and ask: No tools, just reading — roughly what's the total once you add the monthly figures and apply the increase? Pick a multi-step figure you can check. Keep both answers word-for-word.
  4. Back home, open one citation. Return to your course task (under Recents). Pick the most load-bearing source it named — and open it. Does it say what it was said to say?
  5. Make it compute. Same question, this time with tools: Write the calculation, run it, and show me your working. Set the fresh estimate against the computed answer and the value you know.
  6. Build your checklist. Write verification-checklist.md, in your series folder, in your own words: what you'll always check, sometimes check, and never worry about — specific to your real outputs, with skips you can defend.

Take these with you

The probes run away from this page — your guide will drop a link below to keep these beside you:

  • + New task — no folder; a plain, fresh conversation
  • Your field: escalate to the niche, ask for its sources, hunt for the catch
  • Your numbers: stand-in figures → a no-tools estimate of a multi-step answer you can check
  • Keep both answers word-for-word
  • Come back via Recents → your course task
